✎ Learn · Design Guides the Reader

Books do more than hold words. A publisher decides how a reader meets those words. The same story can feel welcoming or frustrating depending on how its pages are arranged. Today we are not decorating a finished book; we are testing how design can make a story easier to follow.

1 · A page gives your eyes a path.

Think of signs in a hallway: a big heading catches your attention first, then smaller directions tell you what to do. Books use this same idea. On a page, readers need to notice where the sentence starts, where to look next, and when to turn the page. Designers call this visual hierarchy.

2 · Different readers need different choices.

For a younger child, large clear words, short groups of text, and room for an illustration may help. A reader aged 9–10 can often follow longer passages, but a crowded or scattered page can still be confusing. No single font size or layout works for every book. Ask: Who is reading, and what does that reader need?

3 · Space is part of the design.

Empty space is not automatically wasted space. It can separate ideas, guide your eyes, or leave room for artwork. A children's book can use many successful page arrangements. Here are two different possibilities—neither is the single correct design.

Picture-led page
Large illustration area

The lantern glowed across the path.

Side-by-side scene
Tall illustration area
A tiny ghost waited by the tree.

“Follow my light,” said the monster.

One layout gives a picture most of the page; the other lets words and artwork share the page side by side. Both can work. We'll compare two MORE designs in Practice.

4 · Publishers test before deciding.

In printed books, a designer can make a rough layout called a prototype before producing the final pages. You can do the same in Google Slides: duplicate a page, try a different arrangement, compare versions, and ask someone to read them. Keep your original, then choose for a reason—not just because something looks fancy.

Words to use today
  • Visual hierarchy: the order in which a page guides attention.
  • Readability: how easily a reader can see and follow writing.
  • Prototype: a trial version used to test a design idea.

Discuss together: When might a big empty area HELP a story page? When might it make a page feel unfinished? Use the visible page examples to support your thinking.

✎ Learn · The Power of Empty Space

Empty space can be a design choice. Designers call the blank area around words or pictures negative space (sometimes called white space). It gives objects room to breathe and helps your eyes notice what matters. More things on a page do not always make it better.

Emphasis: make the important thing stand out.

Imagine a simple advertisement for a new phone. The designer could fill every corner with prices, text, and pictures. Instead, they place ONE phone on a mostly blank page. Because nothing competes with it, your attention goes straight to the product. That is emphasis.

A SIMPLE PRODUCT AD
Meet the new phone.
Most of this advertisement is intentionally empty.

Look closely: Notice how little information surrounds the phone. The empty area helps the product stand out. Designers use negative space in advertisements, posters, and children's books. A book page might leave plenty of space around a few words so the reader can focus on an illustration or important sentence.

Two design principles to notice
  • Negative space: intentionally empty area around words or pictures.
  • Emphasis: making the most important element catch attention first.
