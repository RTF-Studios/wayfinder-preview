✎ Learn · A Proof Is a Test Version

Proof

A version of a publication you inspect before calling it finished. A proof can reveal tiny text, missing words, awkward page breaks or incorrect credits.

Reading path

The order the reader’s eyes should follow. Titles, images, sentences and page turns give clues. Different layouts can succeed when their reading path is clear.

Credit check

The author created the original story. You are its student publisher/designer. Keep the actual author’s name visible rather than claiming you wrote the original story.

Decision from evidence

Notice an issue → test a possible change → compare versions → keep or revise. You do not need a finished book to run a worthwhile proof check.

▣ Perform · Inspect Your Continuing Book

  1. Open the same Slides book. Do not begin a new file. Your book may still have rough pages and placeholders; that is normal.
  2. Pick a small proof sample. Inspect your cover and two consecutive story pages at normal reading size. Note the title, author/publisher credits and where the eye goes first.

✎ Learn · Who Made What?

Publishing someone else’s permitted story is different from authoring it. A good cover can say Written by [actual author] and Published/designed by [student]. Do not invent permission, change the true author’s name, or present another person’s words as your own. If permission or source information is uncertain, mark it for teacher review rather than claiming publication rights.

Example: “The Moonlit Trail” was written by Dana Reyes. Sam chooses fonts and arranges the pages. Dana is the author; Sam is the publisher/designer.

▣ Perform · Test, Then Change

  1. Record one observation. On your existing planning or test-log slide, note a specific issue: where, what a reader might experience, and how you will check it.
  2. Make one purposeful revision. Adjust one page or credit line. If your original version is worth preserving, duplicate the slide first and compare both.
  3. Leave a next-step note. Identify ONE unfinished aspect to investigate when we return to the same book project. You are not expected to finish or publish today.

✎ Learn · A Fair Comparison

When testing two versions, keep the reader and the task as similar as possible. For example, ask the same reader to find the opening sentence in both versions. If one version is clearer, explain what changed. Testing does not prove every reader will react the same way.

✎ Learn · Ready to Share Is Not the Same as Finished

An honest publication decision includes its limits. A draft may be ready for another reader test without being ready for distribution. A good publisher identifies what is working, what remains untested and what should happen next.

Before you enter Creative Labs

✦ Creative Labs · Keep Exploring

After the four checks, choose an experiment. These are optional, ungraded sandboxes; you can change direction or stop at a useful discovery. Work on copies when possible.

Two Covers, One Reader

Try two different arrangements of your existing title and credits. Ask which is easier to understand and why.

Getting started: Duplicate the cover slide first; drag the text boxes on the copy.

Page-Turn Surprise

Move a page division to change when the reader discovers something. Compare both reading experiences.

Getting started: Duplicate the two story slides before changing their order or breaks.

The Tiny-Type Detective

Explore how size and spacing change readability without changing the story's meaning.

Getting started: Duplicate one slide; compare it at normal viewing size, not just when zoomed in.

Proofing Partner

Create a small, friendly checklist for a partner to test reading path, credits and page order.

Getting started: On scratch paper or a blank planning slide, write three observable questions.
