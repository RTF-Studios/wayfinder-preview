✎ Learn · Publishing Is Design

Audience guides your choices.

Ages 5–6 often benefit from short text blocks, large readable type, and illustrations that explain actions. Ages 9–10 can follow longer passages, but still need clear pages and meaningful images.

You are the publisher, not the story author.

You will choose one prepared story, edit small confusing parts if needed, and build its pages in Google Slides. Keep the characters, events, and ending recognizable.

Every design choice needs a purpose.

Layout organizes pages; typography organizes words; illustrations help readers understand scenes. The best design serves the audience—not just your favorite color.

Vocabulary
  • Audience: who will read the book.
  • Typography: how text looks and is arranged.
  • Layout: where words, illustrations, and spaces go.
  • Editing: improving the supplied writing while preserving its story.

Ages 5–6 · The Lantern in the Rain

Nell the rabbit carried a small lantern through the garden. Rain tapped on the leaves. “Come inside!” called her friend Pip from the burrow. But Nell heard a soft squeak under the bench. A tiny mouse was shivering there. Nell held the lantern low so the mouse could see. “Follow my light,” she said. The mouse took one small step, then another. Pip opened the burrow door and spread a dry towel on the floor. Outside, the rain kept falling. Inside, three friends watched the lantern glow.

Ages 5–6 · The Button Parade

Tavi found a shiny blue button beside the path. “A treasure!” Tavi said. At the town parade, a little bear stood beside a wagon. One wheel cover hung loose, and the bear looked worried. “I lost the button that holds it,” the bear said. Tavi looked at the shiny button. It would be fun to keep. But the parade could not begin. “Maybe this belongs to you,” Tavi said. The bear fixed the cover. The wagon rolled, the music started, and Tavi walked at the front with a big smile.

Ages 9–10 · The Map of Quiet Places

Every summer, June drew maps of the neighborhood: the shortcut behind the bakery, the alley where pigeons nested, and the steps that stayed cool at noon. One afternoon, she noticed Mr. Vale beside the playground fence. He held a library book but could not find a quiet place to read. June offered him her map. Then she stopped. The shaded bench she had marked was beside a busy construction site. “My map is wrong,” she admitted. Together they walked past the bakery to a small courtyard. Wind moved through the fig tree, but no traffic reached them. June drew a new star and wrote: “Places change. Good maps should too.”

Ages 9–10 · The Museum of Almost-Forgotten Things

On the first day of the town festival, Ari found a wooden box outside the closed train station. Inside were a cracked compass, a faded photograph, and a ticket from forty years ago. Ari wanted to display them at the school museum. But the librarian pointed at a name on the photograph. “Someone might remember this,” she said. They visited Ms. Ren, who once sold tickets at the station. She recognized the compass: it belonged to her father, a conductor who guided passengers during a winter storm. At the festival, Ari arranged the objects beside Ms. Ren’s account. Visitors lingered to read the story, not just admire the artifacts. As Ari locked the cabinet, the compass needle settled. It still pointed north.

Before you enter Creative Labs

✦ Creative Labs · Keep Designing

Choose a studio experiment. Make and improve something in your book or Design Workshop; keep your versions visible.

Cover Workshop

  1. Duplicate your book cover slide.
  2. Change one element: title size, alignment, or spacing.
  3. Compare both covers and write the reason for your preferred version on the Design Workshop slide.
  4. Keep testing new versions.

Scene Sketch

  1. Pick a scene from your chosen story.
  2. On a duplicated Design Workshop slide, use basic shapes or a rough sketch to plan an illustration.
  3. Label what the image helps your reader understand.
  4. Try an alternative composition.

Typography Test

  1. Duplicate a story page.
  2. Try two readable fonts or two different paragraph layouts.
  3. Show the page to a partner; ask which is easier to read.
  4. Record the evidence and keep improving.

Page Sequence Studio

  1. Add two new slides after the first story page.
  2. Split the next section of the original story between them.
  3. Make sure the order remains correct and the reader has room to breathe.
  4. Read the sequence aloud and revise anything confusing.

Keep trying new versions. Use the Progress Log whenever you make meaningful progress.
