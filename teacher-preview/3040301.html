✎ Learn · Publishing Is Design

Audience guides your choices.

Ages 5–6 often benefit from short text blocks, large readable type, and illustrations that explain actions. Ages 9–10 can follow longer passages, but still need clear pages and meaningful images.

You are the publisher, not the story author.

You will choose one prepared story, edit small confusing parts if needed, and build its pages in Google Slides. Keep the characters, events, and ending recognizable.

Every design choice needs a purpose.

Layout organizes pages; typography organizes words; illustrations help readers understand scenes. The best design serves the audience—not just your favorite color.

Vocabulary
  • Audience: who will read the book.
  • Typography: how text looks and is arranged.
  • Layout: where words, illustrations, and spaces go.
  • Editing: improving the supplied writing while preserving its story.

Ages 5–6 · The Little Monster's Lantern

On Halloween night, Bo carried a pumpkin lantern to the monster parade. Bo had little horns, big feet, and a very tiny roar.

“Squeak!” went Bo.

Two big monsters laughed. “That is not a monster roar!” they said.

Bo hid behind a tree. He wanted to go home.

Then he heard a cry. A baby ghost was lost in the dark.

Bo held up his lantern. “Follow my light,” he said softly.

The ghost floated beside him. Soon they found the parade.

“You helped me!” said the ghost.

One big monster looked down. “I was mean about your roar. I'm sorry.”

Bo smiled. His roar was still small. His kindness was not.

Written by Mara Bell

Ages 5–6 · The Mummy's Missing Bow

Mimi the mummy wore a purple bow to the Halloween party. She had tied it herself.

“Your bow looks silly,” said a goblin.

Other monsters giggled. Mimi pulled her bandages over her face.

At the pumpkin game, the goblin's hat blew into a puddle.

“Oh, no!” he cried.

Mimi could have walked away. Instead, she stretched one long bandage across the puddle and pulled the hat back.

“Thank you,” said the goblin. He looked at Mimi's bow. “I should not have laughed.”

“It hurt my feelings,” Mimi said.

“I'm sorry,” he answered.

Mimi fixed her bow. Then she invited a shy little bat to play.

By the end of the party, everyone had room in the circle.

Written by Ellis Rowan

Ages 9–10 · The Gargoyle on the Gym Roof

At Moonstone School, the Halloween carnival always began when the bell rang at sunset. This year, the bell had gone silent.

Rook, a young gargoyle with chipped stone wings, knew where the bell's missing rope had fallen: onto the steep gym roof. From his perch, he could see the bright red coil caught on a weather vane.

He also knew what the other monsters would say if he offered to help.

“Careful, Rook,” the werewolf twins liked to call. “You might crack!” They had been making the same joke for weeks. Most students laughed. A few looked away.

Rook stayed on the wall until a tiny witch named Fen stopped beneath him. “I saw you looking at the roof,” she said. “Do you think we can get the rope?”

“My wings won't carry me that far,” Rook admitted.

“Then we'll use our heads instead.”

They borrowed a broom, a long ribbon, and a ladder from the decorating crew. Fen steadied the ladder while Rook reached across the roof and hooked the rope with the broom handle. When it slipped, the werewolf twins arrived and caught the dangling ribbon.

Working together, they lowered the rope to the ground.

The bell rang just as the first paper lanterns were lit. The twins started to cheer, then fell quiet.

“We've been making fun of you,” one said. “That wasn't funny.”

“No,” Rook said. “It wasn't.”

The other twin nodded. “Can we help hang the rest of the lanterns?”

Rook considered them for a moment, then pointed to the highest beam. “Start there.”

For the first time that autumn, the twins listened.

Written by Nora Vale

Ages 9–10 · The Witch Who Kept the Names

Every October, the town of Hollowfern held a lantern walk through the old orchard. Students painted their names on glass jars, placed candles inside, and carried them beneath the bare branches.

Nessa, a young witch, loved making the letters curl like vines. She was less fond of the names some classmates had begun writing on scraps of paper and sticking to people's backs.

They called a quiet swamp creature “Mudbrain.” They called a shy skeleton “Rattlebones.” The jokes followed the two students from the classroom to the bus stop.

Nessa had laughed once, hoping nobody would turn the jokes on her. She remembered the swamp creature's face afterward.

On the afternoon of the lantern walk, Nessa found the swamp creature's jar hidden behind the supply cabinet. Someone had scratched out his real name, Ivo, and written “Mudbrain” across the glass.

Nessa's first idea was to scrub the word off and pretend nothing had happened. But Ivo had seen it. He stood in the doorway, his hands tucked under his sleeves.

“They'll just write it again,” he said.

Nessa set down the sponge. “I laughed when they said it before. I'm sorry.”

She asked Ivo what he wanted to do. Together they brought the jar to their teacher, explained what had happened, and made a new lantern. This time Ivo painted his own name, in letters shaped like reeds.

At the walk, Nessa carried her lantern beside his. When another student began the old nickname, she said, “His name is Ivo.” Her voice shook, but she did not stop.

The student fell silent. A few steps later, the shy skeleton moved beside them, lifting a jar painted with tiny silver stars.

Under the trees, their three names glowed. Not one of them was a joke.

Written by Sage Holloway

✎ Learn · Who Gets the Credit?

The author writes the story. The publisher prepares the book for readers. Both roles matter, but they are not the same job.

✍AUTHORWrites the wordsWritten by Nora Vale
➜
▤PUBLISHERBuilds the bookPublished by [Your Name]

Stories, drawings, photos, and music are creative work. Copyright is a law that protects creators and gives them rights over how their work is used. Claiming someone else’s work as your own can violate those rights. Giving credit correctly shows who made the work—but credit alone does not always mean you have permission to use it.
