LEARN · Paint With Words

A setting tells readers WHERE and WHEN events happen. But setting does more than name a place: it shapes what readers expect. Compare “The playground was scary” with “At midnight, one rusty swing creaked through the empty playground.” Both mention a playground, yet the second gives the reader something specific to hear and picture.

Start with the setting. Ask what a visitor could notice: a sound, smell, movement, texture, taste, or sight. Those observations are sensory details. Choose details that support a mood—the feeling or atmosphere the reader experiences. A creaking swing at midnight might suggest unease; children laughing in sunlight could make the same playground feel welcoming.

Notice the writer’s decision: “rusty” and “creaked” suggest age and mystery, while “bright” and “raced” might suggest energy. More adjectives are not automatically better. One precise verb can do more than a long list of vague descriptions.

Goals, obstacles, and conflict

A character’s goal is what the character wants to accomplish. An obstacle is something that makes that goal difficult. The struggle between a goal and an obstacle creates conflict. Obstacles can be people, rules, misunderstandings, fears, or the environment.

WAYFINDER 30402 · MONSTERS CAN BE KIND · DAY 4
LEARN · Make Problems Matter

A story gets interesting when a character wants something but something stands in the way. That is conflict. The character’s goal is what they want; the obstacle prevents them from getting it. Obstacles can be people, misunderstandings, rules, fears, or even bad weather. Not every story needs a villain.

Events need a logical connection. If a crow spreads a false rumor about Moss, and other creatures avoid Moss because they believe it, the rumor is a cause and the avoidance is an effect. “They avoided Moss. The crow talked.” lists events but hides WHY they connect.

Sequence is the order events happen. A cause normally comes before its result, but an author can show a surprising result first and reveal the cause later. Give the reader clues so the order is interesting rather than confusing.

RTF NOTETANK: “The storm does not just happen. It changes what the hero can do.”

WAYFINDER 30402 · MONSTERS CAN BE KIND · DAY 5
LEARN · Improve Through Testing

Writers, editors, game designers, and software developers test work with real users. A reader test works the same way: share a short passage without explaining it first, then ask what the person understood. Their response is feedback—evidence of what worked and what needs attention. A precise observation is more useful than general praise.

A revision is a deliberate improvement, not an order to accept every suggestion. If a reader cannot tell who is talking, you might replace an unclear pronoun with a name. Decide whether the change serves your intended story and audience.

Iteration means test → notice → revise → test again. Ask neutral questions such as “Who was speaking?” rather than “Wasn’t my scene amazing?” Compare the reader’s understanding before and after the revision. Your goal is clearer communication, not agreement or praise.

RTF NOTECAPTAIN: “Let the reader tell us what they saw—not what we hoped they saw.”
REAL-WORLD DESIGN: Editors test writing with readers; game and app teams test with users. All three use specific feedback to improve a design before sharing it widely.
