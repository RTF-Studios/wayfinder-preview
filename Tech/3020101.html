02 · Discover why design matters

Group Learn — A Picture Book Is a Design

Imagine two books telling exactly the same story. One crowds every word onto a page; the other gives the reader room to look, imagine, and pause. Which one would you want to read aloud to a fourth grader?

Long before tablets, illustrated books used page turns to reveal surprises. Today's comics, graphic novels, picture books, and digital publications still control what a reader sees first. A publisher is both a storyteller and a designer: words communicate events; images add mood, action, and meaning.

Reader detective

A scary monster looks lonely at the edge of a playground. What could a close-up illustration tell you that the sentence “Moss was alone” does not?

Design across audiences

A publisher might use smaller type in a reference book for adults, but larger readable type, generous spacing, and expressive illustrations for children ages 9–11. The reader—not the maximum number of words—controls the design.

Image rights matter. A photo found on the internet does not automatically belong to us. For a project we share, use an original picture, teacher-supplied artwork with permission, or an image with a license allowing reuse. Windows + Shift + S captures part of your screen; it does not grant copyright permission.

The page-turn secret: When a character notices something, a page break can make readers wonder what happens next. Publishers also consider complete thoughts, scene changes, picture placement, and how much text their audience can comfortably read.

Talk together: If our monster feels excluded, how might a large lonely illustration and a short sentence work together? What could the very next page reveal?

03 · Watch, predict, and direct

Group Practice — Build With the Teacher

Watch the BenQ demonstration. Before each move, predict how it will change a reader's experience. The teacher shows the actual Google Slides controls you will use.

  1. Insert a text box: Open a blank slide. Choose Insert → Text box, drag a readable rectangle, and type a sentence from the monster story. Ask: Where should the eye begin?
  2. Place the words: Resize and move the text box, leaving space for an illustration. Ask: Does the audience have room to read?
  3. Capture approved artwork: Open the supplied monster illustration library, press Windows + Shift + S, select an illustration, return to Slides and press Ctrl + V.
  4. Resize and position: Drag a corner handle to scale without stretching; place the picture with the words, not on top of them.
  5. Control order: Select the image → Arrange → Order → Send backward (as needed). Demonstrate that layering changes which object covers another. Keep letters clearly readable.
  6. Make a page turn: Add a second slide, move the next sentence into its own text box, and ask where the scene or thought naturally turns. Check both pages at normal zoom.

Transfer challenge: Students explain which demonstration move helped the 4th–5th grade reader most—and why.

05 · Apply it

Perform — Publish a Monster Story

Your fixed story is “Moss and the Rumor.” Design for 4th–5th graders. You are the publisher, NOT the author: do not rewrite the story. You decide where to divide it into pages, how to arrange sentence text boxes, and which existing illustrations belong on each page.

Moss and the Rumor — supplied story

At the edge of the foggy playground lived Moss, a shy green swamp monster. More than anything, Moss wanted someone to play with.

One morning, a crow whispered, “Moss scares anyone who comes near.” Soon the other creatures moved away whenever Moss walked past. Moss sat alone beside the empty swings.

Then a tiny hatchling wandered into the fog and could not find its family. Everyone heard its frightened chirps, but the mist hid the path.

Moss knew the swamp trails by heart. “Follow my footsteps,” Moss said gently. Step by step, Moss led the hatchling home.

The other creatures gathered around. “We believed a rumor instead of getting to know you,” one apologized. “Would you like to play?”

Moss smiled. The next morning, the swings were no longer empty.

Approved illustration library: Open the existing Monsters Can Be Kind illustrated art pages. Use the four existing illustrated pages—choose which monsters and settings to capture with Windows + Shift + S. Do not use random online images.

  1. Open Google Slides and create a new presentation called Unit 2 Storybook — Your First Name.
  2. Copy the supplied story sentences exactly. Use Insert → Text box to arrange the sentences in readable blocks on your first slide.
  3. Decide where the story should turn onto the next page. Create another slide and place the next sentence boxes. You may use additional pages when it helps the audience.
  4. Open the approved art library. Choose illustrations that fit your scenes, snip with Windows + Shift + S, paste with Ctrl + V, resize with a corner handle, and arrange or layer them so words stay readable.
  5. At normal zoom, review the reading order, text size, image placement, and page breaks. Make one audience-focused improvement and confirm Slides has saved your work.

Success check: Your new Google Slides file has the teacher-supplied monster story, at least two thoughtfully arranged pages, and illustrations selected from the supplied library. Layout choices are yours.
